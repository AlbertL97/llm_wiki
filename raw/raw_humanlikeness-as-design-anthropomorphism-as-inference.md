**Summary of Findings:**

This article introduces a new conceptual framework to differentiate between 'humanlikeness' and 'anthropomorphism' within the context of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI).

*   **Humanlikeness as Design:** The authors propose that 'humanlikeness' should be viewed as an intentional design characteristic of robots. This means designers can deliberately imbue robots with features that make them appear human-like.
*   **Anthropomorphism as Inference:** Conversely, 'anthropomorphism' is framed as a cognitive process where humans attribute human-like qualities, intentions, or emotions to non-human entities, including robots. This is seen as an inference made by the user, rather than a direct design attribute of the robot itself.
*   **Conceptual Framework:** The paper's core contribution is a framework that separates the design intent (humanlikeness) from the user's perception and cognitive interpretation (anthropomorphism).
*   **Implications for HRI:** Understanding this distinction is vital for designing robots that foster appropriate levels of trust, engagement, and acceptance. It helps researchers and designers to better predict and manage user responses, particularly concerning the social and psychological aspects of interacting with increasingly sophisticated robots.
*   **Moving Beyond Ambiguity:** By clearly defining these terms, the framework aims to reduce ambiguity in HRI research and practice, leading to more targeted design strategies and more precise experimental analyses.
