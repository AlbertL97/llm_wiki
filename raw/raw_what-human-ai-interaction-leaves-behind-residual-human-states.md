# What Human-AI Interaction Leaves Behind: Residual Human States -- A Trajectory-Based Framework and Multi-Study Analysis

**Authors:** Not specified in the provided text.
**Date:** 2026-10-14 (based on URL format v1 implies first version, assuming context for last updated)

## Comprehensive Summary:

The paper addresses a gap in evaluating Human-AI Interaction (HAI) by focusing not solely on the immediate benefits or performance enhancements an AI agent provides during an interaction, but on the lasting changes it induces in the human user. The authors propose a framework called **Residual Human States (RHS)**, which is trajectory-based. RHS represents the potentially multidimensional human state that persists *after* an interaction with AI, measured relative to an alternative, counterfactual trajectory.

### Key Distinctions and Propositions:

*   **Assisted Output vs. Human-State Transition:** The framework differentiates between the direct output or performance facilitated by the AI and the underlying transition or change in the human's internal state.
*   **Four Empirical Propositions:**
    1.  **Multidimensionality:** Residual human states are not monolithic; they can affect various cognitive, affective, or behavioral dimensions.
    2.  **Interaction-Policy Dependence:** The specific AI interaction strategy or policy employed significantly influences the nature and extent of residual human states.
    3.  **Horizon Dissociation:** The effects of AI interaction might become apparent or change over different time horizons post-interaction, and these effects may differ from the immediate assisted performance.
    4.  **Temporal Persistence:** Residual human states can endure for a significant period after the interaction concludes.

### Central Claim:

Assisted performance, while important, does not solely determine the human state carried forward. Understanding and measuring these residual states is crucial for a holistic evaluation of AI systems.

### Empirical Examination (Four Cases):

1.  **Medical Training Evidence:** Illustrates how a specific AI interaction history can lead to both improved unaided accuracy on some tasks and the transmission of systematic decision biases on others.
2.  **Diagnostic-Learning Study:** Demonstrates that unaided human behavior varies based on different AI support policies. It also highlights that aggregate accuracy metrics cannot distinguish between genuine improvements in discrimination versus shifts in response criteria.
3.  **Classroom Experiment (Generative AI Tutoring):** Showed substantial gains in assisted practice performance with generative AI tutors, but these did not translate into detectable improvements on a subsequent unaided exam, implying residual effects might not always align with immediate assisted gains.
4.  **Longitudinal Reanalysis (Personalized AI Dialogue):** Revealed that residual contrasts can evolve over time (post-interaction horizons) and that these changes can be influenced by both the AI intervention itself and the comparison (counterfactual) trajectory.

### Conclusion:

Evaluating interactive AI systems requires a shift in focus beyond contemporaneous performance. It necessitates considering which human states alternative interaction trajectories produce, relative to counterfactuals, and how durably these states persist.
