Authors: A. T. J. J. van de Ven, R. S. J. te Brake
Date: 2026-08-24
Summary:
This paper examines the deployment of autonomous AI diagnostic agents in sub-Saharan Africa, where the pace of adoption has outstripped governance and oversight. The authors argue that existing AI accountability frameworks are largely clinician-centered and fail to account for the realities of low-resource settings. A significant issue identified is the disparity in patient awareness of these autonomous agents, leading to a 'structural accountability gap'.

The paper synthesizes research on informed consent, algorithmic accountability, and explainable AI to outline three key challenges:
1.  **Patient Awareness Gap:** Patients often do not know they are interacting with or being diagnosed by autonomous AI systems.
2.  **Accountability Gap:** The lack of transparency and awareness creates a void in accountability, making it difficult to assign responsibility when errors occur.
3.  **Contextual Inappropriateness of Existing Frameworks:** Current AI governance and regulatory frameworks are often designed for high-resource settings and assume conditions that are not present in sub-Saharan Africa.

Through case studies from Tanzania (tuberculosis detection), Zambia (diabetic retinopathy and TB screening), and Ghana (mobile health chatbot triage), the paper demonstrates that these issues are already prevalent.

To address these challenges, the authors propose three foundational principles for responsible AI deployment in such contexts:
1.  **Agent-Aware Informed Consent:** Patients must be explicitly informed about the involvement of AI agents in their diagnosis and treatment.
2.  **Human Override as a Structural Requirement:** A mechanism for human oversight and intervention must be built into the system's design and workflow.
3.  **Contextually Adapted Explainability:** Explanations of AI decisions should be tailored to the understanding and needs of both patients and local healthcare providers.

These principles aim to establish a practical minimum standard for developers, health system administrators, and policymakers in regions where formal AI regulation is still developing.
