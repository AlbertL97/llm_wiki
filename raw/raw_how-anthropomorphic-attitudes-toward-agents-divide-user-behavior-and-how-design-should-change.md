Title: How Anthropomorphic Attitudes Toward Agents Divide User Behavior, and How Design Should Change

Description: Studies of conversational agents rely on interviews, surveys, lab tasks, or taxonomies. Whether anthropomorphic naming splits those patterns, and whether replies should change, lack field evidence. We report a field study of a desktop assistant (May-August 2026; n=257).

Key Findings:
- Anthropomorphic naming (e.g., renaming the assistant 'Stance') significantly divides user behavior patterns.
- Users who renamed the agent (45.9% of participants) showed different interaction patterns compared to those who used the default name.
- Renaming users exhibited an introverted, task-only orientation against the default.
- Renaming users greeted more and commanded less.
- The largest gap in the agent's codebook was observed in 'confiding' interactions.
- A revised agent design, which greets and receives affect on relational openings and executes directly on tasking, was preferred by users who renamed the agent.
- In 1,744 judgments (n=129), renamers chose the revised reply significantly more often on being treated as a person and having affect received, and less robustly on willingness to continue, with no difference in task interference.

Methodology:
- Field study of a desktop assistant over four months (May-August 2026).
- Sample size of 257 users.
- Analysis of user behavior using a 128-unit sparse-autoencoder codebook, identity tags, and opening lexicons.
- Statistical analysis including Spearman rank correlation.
- User judgments on revised agent replies.
