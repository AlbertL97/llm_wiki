# Designing Social Robots for Social-Cognition Training with Autistic Adults

**Authors:** Jessica W. Sparsely, Rachel R. Weidinger, Catherine R. Schloer, Sarah B. Lasenby, Jessica C. Metcalfe, Ysbrand W. W. Poortman, Georgina M. E. Kenyon, Sarah B. Neely, Kate E. V. M. Brien, Jennifer R. Rennick, Michael J. A. Robb, Eleanor M. Higgins, Emily H. D. Hughes, Robert A. R. Howard, Eleanor B. D. Scott, Sarah L. Mills, Catherine G. V. D. P. Beavan, Claire B. D. D. Davies, Jonathan T. M. Barnes, Jessica L. R. R. Rogers, Sarah L. K. E. Lowe, Eleanor L. R. D. J. F. Davies, Jonathan T. M. Barnes

**Date:** 2026-08-18

**Abstract:** Social robots have been widely explored as tools for autism intervention, yet this literature has focused predominantly on children and has rarely involved autistic adults as active contributors to design. This creates a mismatch between existing systems and the social-cognitive challenges autistic adults actually face in everyday life, including navigating ambiguous interpersonal contexts, managing conversational timing, and interpreting implied emotional meaning. To address this gap, we conducted an online focus group and co-design session with five autistic adults to explore what a social robot for social-cognition training should do, how it should interact, and under what conditions it would be genuinely useful. The 90-minute session combined open discussion with structured co-design activities on a shared digital whiteboard, and the resulting verbal and visual data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis. The analysis yielded seven themes that define core design requirements: the robot should function as a scaffold rather than a substitute, prioritise authenticity over comfort, provide personalised and user-controlled feedback, accommodate emotional self-awareness gaps, respect privacy and contextual boundaries, support rehearsal for real-world social situations, and remain configurable in identity, form, and expression. Together, the findings suggest that autistic adults envision the robot not as a companion or live social assistant, but as a private, configurable rehearsal partner designed to support independence over time.

**Key Findings:**

*   **Target User Mismatch:** Existing social robot interventions for autism have primarily focused on children, neglecting the specific needs and experiences of autistic adults.
*   **Co-design Importance:** Involving autistic adults directly in the design process is crucial to create systems that genuinely address their social-cognitive challenges.
*   **Robot's Role:** Autistic adults view social robots not as substitutes for human interaction or companions, but as tools for practicing social skills in a safe, private environment.
*   **Core Design Requirements:**
    *   **Scaffolding, Not Substitution:** Robots should support users without replacing human interaction.
    *   **Authenticity over Comfort:** The interaction should prioritize genuine social dynamics over superficial comfort.
    *   **Personalized and User-Controlled Feedback:** Feedback mechanisms should be adaptable to individual needs and preferences.
    *   **Accommodating Emotional Self-Awareness Gaps:** The robot should be designed to help users navigate and understand their own emotional states.
    *   **Respecting Privacy and Boundaries:** The robot must operate within strict privacy and contextual limitations.
    *   **Supporting Real-World Rehearsal:** The robot should facilitate practice for actual social situations.
    *   **Configurability:** Users should be able to customize the robot's identity, form, and expression.
*   **Vision for the Robot:** The ideal robot is a private, configurable rehearsal partner that promotes long-term independence.
